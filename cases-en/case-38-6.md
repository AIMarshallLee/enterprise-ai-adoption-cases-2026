# Case 38.6 (United States, FAILURE CASE): Legal Services — New York lawyers use ChatGPT for research, submit fictitious case law to the court, and are found to have acted in bad faith and fined US\$5,000

**Industry / scenario**: (Size undisclosed: headcount not disclosed in the sources) New York law firm Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C. The scenario is legal research and drafting for litigation filings.

**Pain point**: The firm practiced mainly in New York state courts, and its research tool, Fastcase, had limited access to federal case law. Attorney Schwartz needed case law to oppose, in federal court, the airline's motion to dismiss on the grounds that the limitations period had expired; he had heard about ChatGPT in the news and mistakenly believed it was a "super search engine."

**Why it failed**: The court found that attorney Steven A. Schwartz used ChatGPT for legal research. When the judge asked in court, "But ChatGPT was not supplementing your research. It was your research, correct?", he answered, "Correct." The lawyers submitted non-existent judicial opinions and fake quotations, and continued to maintain that the cases existed after court orders called their existence into question. Peter LoDuca, the attorney who signed the filing, had not read any of the cases cited in the submission he signed. The court also noted that there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable AI tool for assistance, but that existing rules impose a "gatekeeping role" on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings.

**Outcome**: On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel of the US District Court for the Southern District of New York found bad faith on the part of the two lawyers individually, imposed a US\$5,000 penalty on the two of them and their firm jointly and severally, payable within 14 days, and ordered them within 14 days to send a letter to their client Roberto Mata (enclosing the order), and also to send a letter individually to each judge falsely identified as the author of the six fake opinions "Varghese," "Shaboon," "Petersen," "Martinez," "Durden" and "Miller."

**Lessons**: Case law and citations produced by generative AI must be checked one by one against authoritative databases; the firm's policies should state clearly that whoever signs is responsible; when challenged, correct errors promptly rather than persisting in them. According to Fortune, in the two years that followed at least 139 legal filings contained AI-fabricated citations, with more than 30 in May 2025 alone (media figure; the article does not state the source of the count).
